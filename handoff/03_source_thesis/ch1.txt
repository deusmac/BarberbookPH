## Introduction
P Almost every town in the Philippines has at least one barbershop. Some are small, one-chair shops beside a sari-sari store, while others are air-conditioned barber lounges with four or five barbers working at the same time. Whatever the size, the barbershop is a place where men and boys go regularly, usually every few weeks, to have their hair cut, their beard trimmed, or sometimes just to relax and talk with the barber. For many Filipino customers, the neighborhood barbershop is not only a business but also a familiar part of their routine.
P Even with this familiarity, the way most local barbershops handle their customers has stayed almost the same for years. The common practice is first come, first served. A customer walks in, looks at how many people are already waiting, and then decides whether to stay or come back another time. On busy days such as weekends, paydays, and the days before school opening, graduation, or the town fiesta, the waiting time can reach [[one to two hours (replace with the actual waiting time you observed or were told by the owner)]]. Some shops accept reservations through Facebook Messenger or text message, but these are usually handled by the owner using his personal phone, and the list of appointments is written in a notebook or simply remembered.
P At the same time, Filipinos are among the most active internet users in the world. Based on the Digital 2026 report for the Philippines, there were around 98 million internet users in the country, or about 83.8 percent of the population, and around 137 million cellular mobile connections (Kemp, 2026). Many customers already use their phones to order food, book rides, and pay bills. Because of this, it is reasonable to expect that they would also welcome a simple way of booking a haircut online, in the same way that they already book other everyday services.
P Another concern that is often overlooked is the communication between the customer and the barber. A haircut is a very personal service, and the result depends a lot on whether the barber understood what the customer wanted. Customers often describe their preferred style using general words such as "semi-kalbo," "clean cut," "short on the sides," or "the usual," or they show a photo from social media at the last minute. When the barber misunderstands the request, the customer may go home unhappy, and the shop may lose a regular client. Price and Arnould (1999) pointed out that in personal services such as hairstyling, the relationship and understanding between the service provider and the client have a strong effect on the client's satisfaction and loyalty.
P Because of these concerns, the proponents propose **BarberBook PH: An Online Appointment and Haircut Preference System for Local Barbershops**. It is a web-based system that allows customers to reserve a time slot in a barbershop, choose a preferred barber, select a hairstyle from a photo gallery, and save their personal haircut preferences so that the barber can review them before the appointment. On the side of the barbershop, the owner will have a dashboard for managing barbers, working hours, available slots, and the daily list of appointments. Through this study, the proponents hope to give local barbershops a practical and affordable tool that fits the way Filipino customers already use their phones.
## Background of the Study
P The barbershop business belongs to the large group of micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) in the country. According to the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI, n.d.), there were 1,246,373 business enterprises in the Philippines in 2023, and 99.63 percent of these were MSMEs. Micro enterprises alone made up 90.43 percent of all establishments, and MSMEs as a whole generated 66.97 percent of the country's total employment. The sector of "other service activities," where personal services such as barbershops and salons are usually classified, was among the top five industries with 87,197 MSME establishments (DTI, n.d.). These numbers show that small service businesses like barbershops are an important part of the local economy, even if each individual shop is small.
P Most of these small businesses operate with limited resources. They usually cannot afford commercial booking software that charges monthly subscription fees, and many owners have no formal training in record keeping. A study of small entrepreneurs in Davao City found that most of them used ordinary notebooks as their main tool for recording income, expenses, and payables instead of standard ledgers (Patayon, 2016). Santos et al. (2024) also found that MSME owners in a Philippine city were very aware of the records required of them by law, but many still could not comply because of their limited accounting knowledge. These findings are similar to what the proponents observed in local barbershops, where the only record of appointments and customers is often a handwritten list.

P From these initial observations, the proponents identified several problems. First, customers experience long and unpredictable waiting times, especially during peak days. Maister (1985) explained that waiting feels longer to customers when it is uncertain and unexplained, and Taylor (1994) found that delays lower the customer's overall evaluation of a service. Second, reservations made through chat or text can overlap because there is no single schedule that the owner and all the barbers can see. Third, the customer's haircut preference is not written down anywhere, so the barber has to ask the same questions every visit, and misunderstandings still happen. Fourth, customers sometimes forget their appointment, and the reserved slot goes to waste. Lastly, the shop has no organized customer records, so the owner cannot easily tell how many customers came in during the week, who the regular customers are, or which barber is most requested.
P Online appointment systems have already helped other service industries deal with similar problems. In health care, appointment scheduling has been studied for many years as a way of balancing the waiting time of patients with the idle time of doctors (Cayirli & Veral, 2003; Gupta & Denton, 2008), and text message reminders have been shown to increase attendance at scheduled appointments (Guy et al., 2012). In the Philippines, online appointment and reservation systems have been developed for a government regional office (Marasigan, 2025), university client services (Concepcion et al., 2026), resorts (Balintag et al., 2025; Ramento et al., 2024), and salons in Pangasinan (Carino et al., 2025). However, the proponents found only a few local studies that focused specifically on barbershops, and even fewer that included a way to record and communicate the haircut preference of the customer.
P This is the gap that BarberBook PH aims to address. The proponents believe that combining online booking with a hairstyle gallery and saved haircut preferences will not only reduce waiting time and scheduling conflicts but will also improve the quality of the haircut service itself, because the barber will already know what the customer wants before the cut starts. The system will also give the owner a simple digital record of appointments and customers, which can later help in making better decisions about staffing, shop hours, and promotions.
## Theoretical Framework
P This study is anchored on four theories and models. The first two explain why customers and barbershop owners would accept and continue using the proposed system, the third explains why reducing uncertain waiting is important, and the fourth provides the basis for evaluating the system.
H3 Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
P The Technology Acceptance Model was introduced by Davis (1989) to explain why people accept or reject a new information technology. According to TAM, two beliefs mainly influence a person's intention to use a system. The first is *perceived usefulness*, or the degree to which a person believes that using the system will improve his or her performance. The second is *perceived ease of use*, or the degree to which a person believes that using the system will be free of effort. Venkatesh et al. (2003) later expanded this idea in the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which added factors such as social influence and facilitating conditions.
P In this study, TAM explains why BarberBook PH must be both useful and easy to use. If customers feel that booking online saves them time and that the steps are simple, they are more likely to use the system instead of just walking in. The same is true for barbers and the shop owner, who need to feel that the dashboard makes their work easier and not more complicated. This is the reason why the proponents kept the screens plain and the booking process short, as shown in the proposed user interface.
H3 DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model
P DeLone and McLean (2003) proposed that the success of an information system can be understood through six connected dimensions: system quality, information quality, service quality, use or intention to use, user satisfaction, and net benefits. A system that works well and gives accurate information is more likely to be used, and continued use leads to satisfaction and to benefits for both the users and the organization.
P This model supports the idea that BarberBook PH should not only work from a technical point of view. It should also give accurate schedules and customer information, satisfy its users, and bring actual benefits to the barbershop, such as fewer scheduling conflicts, fewer forgotten appointments, and more organized customer records. User satisfaction, one of the dimensions in this model, is also one of the criteria that will be used to evaluate the system.
H3 Psychology of Waiting Lines
P Maister (1985) described several principles about how people experience waiting. Among these are that unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time, that uncertain waits feel longer than known and finite waits, and that unexplained waits feel longer than explained waits. Taylor (1994) supported this by showing that service delays affect the feelings of customers and lower their evaluation of the service.
P These principles are important to this study because one of the main purposes of BarberBook PH is to replace uncertain walk-in waiting with a known appointment time. When customers know exactly when they will be served and receive a confirmation and reminder, the experience becomes more predictable and less frustrating, even if they still have to wait a few minutes at the shop.
H3 ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Model
P The International Organization for Standardization (ISO, 2011) published ISO/IEC 25010 as a model for describing and evaluating the quality of software products. The model has two parts. The product quality model includes characteristics such as functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, and reliability, while the quality in use model includes effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, freedom from risk, and context coverage. A revised version of the product quality model was released in 2023, which renamed some characteristics, such as usability becoming interaction capability, and added safety as a new characteristic (ISO, 2023).
P In this study, the ISO/IEC 25010 model serves as the basis of the evaluation instrument that will measure the acceptability of BarberBook PH in terms of functionality, usability, efficiency, reliability, and user satisfaction, as stated in the fifth research question. ## Definition of Terms

DEF Administrator Dashboard.|The main page seen by the barbershop owner after logging in. In this study, it shows the daily queue, barber schedules, available slots, the style gallery, and simple booking reports.

DEF Usability.|The degree to which a system can be used by its users to achieve their goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction (ISO, 2011). In this study, it refers to how easy it is for customers, barbers, and the owner to learn and use the system.

DEF Walk-in Customer.|A customer who goes to the barbershop without an appointment and waits for his turn.
DEF Weighted Mean.|A statistical tool that computes the average of responses where each response is multiplied by its assigned weight. In this study, it is used to interpret the results of the acceptability evaluation.
